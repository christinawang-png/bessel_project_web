1. Introduction

To increase imaging speed and sharpness, modern fluorescence microscopes increasingly rely on engineered point spread functions (PSFs) and specialized beams to image large 3D volumes. In these systems, a single camera frame is the result of a complex forward operator that maps a 3D fluorophore distribution V(x,y,z) to a 2D intensity image I(x,y). Accurately modeling this operator is important for experimental design, quantitative interpretation, and generating realistic synthetic data for inverse models. Hand‑written simulators, typically in MATLAB or C++, require calibrated PSFs and many optical parameters and usually neglect experimental artifacts such as device‑specific warp and acquisition noise.

In a simplified model, image formation can be written as a 3D convolution with the system PSF h(x,y,z), followed by axial integration. For a discretized volume V ∈ RZ × H × W and a measured or simulated PSF h, an ideal noise‑free projection is

U(x,y) = ∑z (V * h)(x,y,z),

where * denotes lateral convolution. In practice the measured image deviates from U due to a static optical warp W : R2 → R2 (e.g. lens or scan distortion) and acquisition noise. We model the observed intensity as

I(x,y) = N(U(W(x,y))),

where N(·) denotes the combined effect of gain fluctuations, scan jitter, photon shot noise, and detector read noise.

This project asks whether a moderately sized neural network can learn this forward operator from synthetic data generated by a known PSF‑based simulator. Given a 3D volume V, we generate paired 2D images using a slice‑by‑slice FFT implementation of the convolution above, optionally followed by a fixed warp W and a realistic noise model. We then train networks Fθ to approximate either the device‑like output I(x,y) or its unwarped counterpart U(x,y). Our baseline model is a 3D–2D convolutional network that is translation‑equivariant in the lateral dimensions.

However, standard CNNs have limited ability to represent global geometric distortions such as W using only local convolutions and finite receptive fields. To address this, we also explore augmenting the baseline CNN with an explicit, global warp module Gφ implemented as a small coordinate‑based MLP:

Û(x,y) = Fθ(V),    Î(x,y) = Û( Gφ(x,y) ).

This factorization reflects the underlying physics (convolution + warp) and allows us to separately analyze how well the network learns each part. Throughout the paper we compare (i) the plain CNN, (ii) the CNN with an explicit learned warp, and (iii) a variant with additional positional encodings, and study where these models succeed or fail to reproduce the known simulated forward operator.

2. Background and Related Work

2.1 Fluorescence Microscopy and PSF-Based Models

Fluorescence microscopy records emitted light from fluorophores excited by an illumination beam. Two‑photon microscopy [1] uses near‑simultaneous absorption of two lower‑energy photons, reducing scattering and phototoxicity and enabling deep tissue imaging. Confocal microscopy [4] rejects out‑of‑focus light via a pinhole, allowing 3D imaging by scanning in depth.

Many modern techniques modify the excitation pattern or beam shape. Super‑resolution methods use structured illumination or stochastic activation to break the diffraction limit [2][3], while Bessel and other elongated beams [5] create an axially extended focus so that a single lateral scan encodes information from a thick volume. With multiple tilt angles, synthetic aperture schemes [6] can recover 3D structure from a small number of long‑beam projections.

In all cases, the mapping from fluorophore density to measured image is a non‑trivial forward operator, typically modeled as a 3D convolution with a point spread function (PSF) plus noise and system‑specific distortions. Classical reconstruction approaches, such as Richardson–Lucy deconvolution under a Poisson noise model [7], iteratively invert this operator but can be slow, sensitive to initialization, and prone to amplifying noise, especially for complex beams.

2.2 Learned Physical Operators

Rather than coding the forward operator analytically, recent work explores learning physical operators with neural networks. Fourier Neural Operators (FNOs) learn mappings between function spaces by mixing information in the frequency domain and can accelerate PDE solution compared to classical solvers [8]. In imaging, most deep‑learning methods focus on inverse problems—denoising, deblurring, and deconvolution—assuming a fixed known forward model, from unrolled Richardson–Lucy schemes [9] to generic 2D reconstruction networks [10].

However, an ideal calibrated forward model can be a limitation when the optics are imperfect or hard to characterize. A learned device‑specific forward model could be inserted into reconstruction pipelines to better match the true behavior of a particular microscope. Comparatively few studies have evaluated how well compact neural networks can approximate realistic optical forward models for a specific system, including both PSF‑based blur and geometric warp. Our work targets this gap: we treat a calibrated PSF simulator as ground truth and investigate how closely different architectures can reproduce its behavior, and how explicitly modeling geometric warp affects that approximation.

Methodology

3.1 Data and Preprocessing

We base our experiments on the MiniVess dataset [10], which contains 70 3D two‑photon fluorescence microscopy volumes of mouse cerebral vasculature. Each raw volume is provided as a NIfTI file of size 512 × 512 × Z voxels with varying depth Z. To obtain a dataset suitable for learning a forward operator with fixed input size, we apply the following pre‑processing steps:

  • Z‑normalization: For each volume we clip along the axial dimension to a common depth z = 33 slices. Volumes with fewer slices are discarded; volumes with more slices are either center‑cropped in z, or split into multiple chunks if Z >= 2z.
  • XY cropping: To reduce memory and increase the number of effective training examples, we crop each 512 × 512 field of view into four non‑overlapping 256 × 256 patches. Each patch is treated as an independent 3D volume of size 33 × 256 × 256.
  • Intensity normalization: Within each 3D patch, we normalize the voxel intensities by the maximum value in that patch, so that inputs to the network lie roughly in [0,1].

Below are 2 video examples of a processed 3D chunk, rendered and viewed by Napari.

After pre‑processing, the training set consists of N volumes V(i) ∈ RZ × H × W with Z = 33 and H = W = 256. We randomly split these into 80% training and 20% validation volumes, and use the same indices to construct ideal (noise‑free) and noisy (device‑like) targets for evaluation.

3.2 PSF-Based Forward Simulation

To simulate the microscope forward operator we use a measured 3D point spread function (PSF) h(x,y,z) obtained from a MATLAB calibration pipeline for a Bessel‑like beam, originally modified from the optics simulation code from Zhao et. al's paper [6]. The PSF is stored as a MATLAB .mat file with dimensions Zpsf × Hpsf × Wpsf. Below is the example PSF beam we trained upon. It is a long and thin beam with variable intensity in Z.

We transpose axes as needed to match the volume convention and normalize by its maximum value. For a given volume V, we approximate the continuous 3D convolution

(V * h)(x,y,z) = ∑x',y',z' V(x',y',z') h(x-x',y-y',z-z')

using a slice‑by‑slice FFT‑based implementation. For each axial slice z = 1, …, Z, we compute

  1. Zero‑pad the volume slice Vz(y,x) and the corresponding PSF slice hz(y,x) into larger arrays A and B.
  2. Compute C = ℱ-1(ℱ(A) ċ ℱ(B)), where ℱ denotes the 2D Fourier transform.
  3. Crop the central H × W region of C to obtain the convolved slice.

Summing over z yields an ideal 2D projection:

U(y,x) = ∑{z=1}^{Z} (V * h)z(y,x).

Because FFT‑based linear convolution with zero padding tends to darken the outermost pixels, we optionally crop a thin border from the projection and pad it back using edge replication to reduce visible boundary artifacts, while keeping the output size fixed at 256 × 256.

3.3 Static Warp and Noise Model

Real microscopes exhibit geometric distortions due to lens aberrations, scan non‑linearities, and imperfect alignment. We introduce a simple static warp W : (x,y) → (x',y') to mimic this behavior. In normalized coordinates (x,y) ∈ [-1,1]^2, we use a radial barrel/pincushion model

r = √(x2 + y2),    (x',y') = (x,y) (1 + k r2),

where k controls the strength and sign of the distortion (k > 0 for barrel, k < 0 for pincushion).

We apply this warp using scipy.ndimage.map_coordinates with reflective boundary conditions and a smooth falloff toward the image edges to produce a device‑like warped version of the ideal image: Uw(y,x) = U(W(x,y)).

To approximate acquisition noise, we apply three additional perturbations:

  • Per‑frame gain fluctuation: multiply the image by a global random factor g ∼ N(1, σg2)
  • Per‑line jitter: for each scan line y, apply a small random lateral shift Δxy ∼ N(0,σj2) using linear interpolation, representing mechanical or timing jitter during scanning.
  • Photon and read noise: interpret the intensity as an expected photon count, draw from a Poisson distribution, and add a small Gaussian read noise: C ∼ Poisson(λ = s Uw), I = C + N(0,σr2), then rescale by 1/s.

The final synthetic measurement I(y,x) serves as the target for learning the device‑like forward operator. In addition, we retain U(y,x) as an ideal, unwarped reference image for analysis.

3.4 Neural Network Models

3.4.1 Baseline 3D–2D CNN

Our baseline model is a compact 3D–2D convolutional network that directly maps a volume V ∈ R1 × Z × H × W to a 2D image Î ∈ R1 × H × W. The architecture consists of:

  • Two 3D convolutional layers with kernel size 3×3×3 and ReLU activations, producing a feature map of size C × Z × H × W.
  • Global average pooling along the axial dimension Z, yielding a 2D feature map C × H × W.
  • Two 2D convolutional layers with ReLU activations, followed by a final 2D convolution to produce a single‑channel output image.

The model is trained to minimize a combination of L1 and L2 losses between its prediction Î and the synthetic measurement I:

Lpixel(Î, I) = α   ‖Î - I‖1 + (1 - α)   ‖Î - I‖22.

with α = 0.5 in our experiments. This baseline implicitly attempts to learn both the PSF‑based blur and any static warp present in the data, using only local convolutions and finite receptive fields.

3.4.2 CNN with Explicit Warp Module

To better reflect the structure of the physical forward operator, we introduce a second model that explicitly factorizes optical warp. A shared 3D–2D CNN backbone Fθ first predicts an unwarped image Û = Fθ(V). A separate coordinate‑based MLP Gφ takes sinusoidal coordinate encodings (x,y) ∈ [-1,1]^2 and outputs a displacement field (Δx,Δy). The final prediction is obtained by sampling Û at the warped coordinates using a differentiable resampler:

Î(x,y) = Û(x + Δx(x,y),y + Δy(x,y)).

In practice, we implement Gφ as a small MLP with one hidden layer of width 16 and tanh activations, and use torch.nn.functional.grid_sample to apply the warp. The model is trained end‑to‑end with the same pixelwise loss as the baseline.

During evaluation, we compare both the warped prediction Î against the noisy, warped target I and the unwarped prediction Û against the ideal target U, allowing us to separately assess how well the model learns the photometric and geometric components of the forward operator.

3.5 Training Details

All models are implemented in PyTorch and trained with the Adam optimizer using a learning rate 10−3, batch size 2, 100 epochs for the basic CNN, and 250 epochs for the CNN with the warp module. We monitor the validation loss and Pearson correlation between predictions and targets, and select the checkpoint with the lowest validation loss for detailed analysis. Unless otherwise noted, no additional data augmentation beyond the cropping described in Section 3.1 is applied.

4.1 Quantitative Evaluation

We evaluate all final trained models on the validation set of MiniVess volumes using Pearson correlation between predicted and target images.

CNN + warp (with enc) (Intermed vs.Ideal) Warped + noisy 0.9453

Overall, the plain CNN performs well when the targets are unwarped projections, even when there is noise, achieving high correlation to U. When trained directly on the noisy, warped targets I, however, the CNN struggles: it tends to predict an image that is visually closer to an unwarped projection than to the warped ground truth.

By contrast, the CNN + warp model achieves substantially higher correlation to the noisy, warped targets. The shared 3D–2D backbone produces an unwarped prediction Û whose correlation to U is comparable to, and in many cases slightly better than, the plain CNN trained on warped targets. The learned warp module then brings Û into good agreement with the device‑like images I, reducing residual structured error due to the known static warp. In other words, factorizing the operator into a photometric component and a geometric component improves both tasks: learning the ideal PSF‑based projection and matching the warped device output.

Additionally, We also trained a variant of the warp model with Fourier positional encodings onto the 3D input coordinates. This model performs slightly worse on validation metrics than the no‑encoding warp model, but still clearly outperforms the plain CNN baseline on warped targets. The degradation is small but consistent, suggesting that in this regime the added coordinate dependence does not provide a net benefit.

4.3 Effect of Positional Encodings

Motivated by work on Fourier features, we also augmented the 3D volume with sinusoidal encodings of the (x,y,z) coordinates. In principle this should increase the network's effective frequency capacity by providing fixed high‑frequency basis functions. In practice, adding positional encodings slightly degraded validation performance for the CNN + warp model: correlations were lower than for the version without encodings, although still well above the plain CNN baseline on warped targets.

A likely reason is that our simulated forward operator is already well matched to the translation‑equivariant bias of convolutions: apart from a fixed warp, it is dominated by shift‑invariant PSF blur and integration. Extra coordinate channels break this symmetry and add capacity, making it easier to overfit location‑specific noise and boundary effects without clear benefit for approximating the underlying physics.

5. Limitations and Future Work

This work has several limitations. The forward simulator uses a single measured PSF and a simple static radial warp; real microscopes can have space‑varying PSFs, depth‑dependent aberrations, and more complex distortions. Our models are therefore only validated against this specific synthetic operator, not against real measurements. The training set is also modest in size and the targets are noisy, which likely contributes to the remaining loss of fine vessel structure, especially at high frequencies and near image boundaries. Finally, the warp module is an unconstrained MLP whose displacement field is only indirectly regularized by the pixel loss, leading to over‑smooth or locally inaccurate warps where supervision is weak.

Future work could improve both modeling and data. Architecturally, a richer 2D decoder with U‑Net–style skip connections and explicit edge or Fourier losses may better preserve high‑frequency detail while maintaining global structure. The warp could be replaced by a lower‑dimensional, more interpretable parameterization (e.g., low‑order polynomials or splines) with explicit smoothness and magnitude regularization. On the data side, extending the simulator to multiple PSFs and validating learned forward models directly against real microscope images would provide a stronger test of generalization and practical utility.
